Sam opens a long catalog of courses about agents. The page is a wall of product names. Some lessons want a terminal. Some want a paid account. Some say the real skill is a command that will be different next month. Sam has never programmed. Sam closes the tab.

You are in Sam's seat. This course is the short path that catalog was standing in front of. It trains one skill: you can look at a piece of work and say whether a chat is enough, whether a fixed recipe is enough, or whether something has to choose the next step, check the result, and remember yesterday. By the last lesson you will design that something on a worksheet. You will not install it.

## What a lesson page is for

Every lesson after this one has the same shape, so you can spend your attention on the idea.

First you meet a story. The story is usually Sam trying to make a one-page Sunday study digest from three links. The story comes before the name of the idea, because a name is easier to remember once you have seen the trouble it points at.

Then the page names the idea in a short section. That name is the word the glossary will keep. You do not need to memorize a second vocabulary for the same thing.

Then the page names one mistake people make. The mistake is in a shaded block. Read it even if you think you would not make it. Most of the mistakes in this course feel reasonable the first time.

Then you get two checks. Each check is closed. The question is visible. The answer is hidden until you open it. Write an answer in a note, or say it out loud, and only then open the disclosure. Opening it first turns the check into another paragraph.

Last, a recap of four lines. The recap is a reminder, not a second lesson. If the recap is the first thing that makes sense, go back to the story and read once more. That second pass is part of the time on the label.

## What you need

You need a browser. You need enough quiet to read a page and answer two questions. A free chat on the web is useful later, when you want to watch a model sound sure. You can finish every lesson without opening one.

You do not type commands. You do not create an account on this site. If you tap "Mark read," this browser stores that mark on this machine. Another browser will not see it. That is the whole memory of the site.

The path is seventeen lessons, counting this one. The minute label on each page is seat time: reading, looking at a picture when there is one, and answering the checks. The labels add up to a bit more than six hours. You can stop between lessons. You should not skip ahead, because each page tells you what it assumes you already met.

## How to read a hard sentence

Some sentences name a test you could apply with a pen. When you meet one, stop and apply it to Sam's digest before you continue. For example, a later lesson will say a finish line has to be something a second person could mark pass or fail. When you get there, take Sam's wish "make a good summary" and try to mark it. You will not be able to. That stuck moment is the lesson working.

If a picture appears, read the caption and the words inside the picture. The picture is not decoration. It is the shape of the idea: a desk, three kinds of help, one beat, four ways the next beat starts, six parts, a writer and a grader, or four nested layers. There are seven pictures in the whole course. Everything else is text on purpose. Text is what you can reread at your own speed.

## A second look at the catalog

Go back to the catalog Sam closed. One course says you will be productive in a weekend if you learn two coding tools at once. Another says the important skill is a schedule command. Both are talking to someone who already drives an agent. They are not wrong for that person. They are the wrong first door for you.

This course keeps the ideas that stay useful after the commands change. A model continues text. It sees only what is placed in front of it. Some help waits for you, some help follows a fixed recipe, and some help chooses a next step. A goal you can test is different from a wish. A tool has a result you can point at. One pass can end because the model says "Done!" even when the test was never applied. Something has to start the next pass. The person who writes should not be the only person who grades. Yesterday has to be written down outside the model. And you still own the decision about what the work is for, and the decision about what is allowed to go out into the world.

You will meet those ideas one at a time. This page does not teach them yet. If a term in that list is fuzzy, leave it fuzzy. The lesson that owns it will tell a story first.

## What to do when you want to quit

The middle of the course walks one Sunday in slow motion. It can feel slower than a video that shows a finished agent in two minutes. The slowness is the point. A two-minute demo hides the moment the model claims it read a page it never opened. You are here to notice that moment and name it.

When a check feels hard, that is a signal to reread the story, not to open the answer and move on. When a lesson feels easy, still answer the checks. Easy ideas are the ones people skip, and then they cannot tell a chat from an agent a week later.

You are ready for lesson 1 when you can say three things: this course trains judgment about agents, each page has a story and two checks, and you need nothing installed.

## A practice page, before lesson 1

Take this page itself as the practice object. Point at the story: Sam and the catalog. Point at the name of the idea: a lesson has a fixed shape, and the course needs nothing installed. Point at the mistake you are about to read. Point at the two checks, still closed. Point at the recap, and notice it is shorter than the story. If you can point at those five pieces here, you will recognize them when the topic is a model, a desk, or a Sunday clock.

Then make a one-line plan for the week. Write the lesson numbers you will read, and leave a blank beside each for the check you found hardest. The blank is not a grade. It is a way to see which idea needs a second pass. A second pass on a short page is faster than a confused leap into the next one. The minute labels assume that second pass. They are not a speed target.

When a friend asks what you are studying, answer with the skill, not with a product name. You are learning to tell a waiting chat from a chooser, to write a test a second person could mark, and to say who must still approve a risky send. That answer is true on lesson 0 as a promise, and it becomes something you can demonstrate on lesson 16. You do not owe anyone a demo in between.

<Misconception>

I have to learn the products before the ideas.

</Misconception>

The products are real, and later you may want one. They are a bad first lesson because their buttons change. The ideas in this course are what you use to read a new product when you meet one. A button named for a finish line will make sense because you already know what a finish line is. A button that only says "auto" will look empty, because you will know to ask what it checks and when it stops.

<Check question="What do you install before lesson 1?">

Nothing. A browser is enough. A free chat is optional, and this site does not require an account.

</Check>

<Check question="When do you open a check answer?">

After you have written or said your own answer. The answer stays closed so the check can be a check.

</Check>
